# Flood-aware rotation strategies for lowland rice fields

These are options for discussion with the farmer and a local technician. They are not planting instructions.

## S0 — Current pattern: wet-season rice → dry-season rice or ratoon

**Why it is shown.** This is the farmer's current baseline. It keeps rice in the field through the wet season and repeats rice or ratoon in the dry season.

**Evidence.** The app's NASA POWER 2001–2025 climatology identifies January–April as the wettest months and calculates crop water use as ETc = Kc × ET0. For the worked Daule dry-season calendar in the project specification, rice irrigation need is about 748 mm (June–September). Rice Kc values are from FAO-56 Table 12: [FAO-56](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e0b.htm). The rice climate and poor-drainage suitability fields come from [FAO ECOCROP](https://ecocrop.apps.fao.org/ecocrop/srv/en/dataSheet?id=1574).

**Benefits.** Familiar management and an established rice market.

**Information gaps.** Field elevation, flood depth and duration, drainage capacity, rice variety and maturity date, actual irrigation access and local prices.

**Risks for local review.** Young rice can be damaged by prolonged flooding; repeating rice does not break the rice monoculture; dry-season rice can require substantial irrigation. Check local flood warnings, seed availability, pests and disease history.

## S1 — Rice → soybean in the dry season

**Why it is shown.** Soybean is a legume, so it can break the rice-after-rice sequence. INIAP advises soybean immediately after rice or maize harvest to use remaining soil moisture, only when conditions are dry; in lower Guayas Basin areas including Babahoyo, dry-season soybean may need irrigation.

**Evidence.** In the project specification's Daule example, soybean irrigation need is about 553 mm in June–September, about 195 mm (about 26%) less than rice using the same NASA POWER climate inputs and FAO-56 Kc values. INIAP documents the local rice–soybean timing and the need to avoid wet planting conditions: [INIAP soybean establishment guide](https://tecnologia.iniap.gob.ec/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/4cultivo-11.pdf). A Babahoyo study also describes wet-season rice followed by dry-season soybean: [Caicedo-Camposano et al.](https://killkana.ucacue.edu.ec/index.php/killkana_tecnico/article/view/472).

**Benefits.** Breaks rice monoculture, adds a legume to the rotation, and may lower modeled dry-season irrigation need relative to rice.

**Information gaps.** Whether floodwater has fully receded, soil trafficability, seed and buyer availability, irrigation volume and timing, salinity, disease pressure and expected price.

**Risks for local review.** Soybean is not a flooded-field crop. Do not sow into waterlogged soil. Late planting can face water shortage; wet conditions increase fungal disease and may reduce grain quality. Confirm a dry planting window and irrigation plan with a local technician.

## S2 — Avoid wet-season rice on low flood-prone plots; use a water-tolerant cover or fallow, then dry-season rice with irrigation

**Why it is shown.** On plots known to flood, this option avoids placing young rice in the field during the riskiest wet months. It is a risk-management scenario, not a forecast or a universal replacement for rice.

**Evidence.** The app counts crop exposure during January–April using NASA POWER monthly rainfall and displays the wetter-than-normal El Niño setting explicitly as a scenario. Two possible covers for review are *Sesbania rostrata* and *Azolla*: IRRI reports that *S. rostrata* can grow under flooded and dry conditions as a green manure, while FAO describes Azolla growing in standing water in rice fields and being incorporated as green manure. Sources: [IRRI Green Manure](https://books.irri.org/9789712202322_content.pdf) and [FAO nutrient-management guidance](https://www.fao.org/4/a0443e/a0443e.pdf).

**Benefits.** Reduces exposure of a rice crop during the wettest months; can avoid a bare field if an appropriate cover is locally feasible; a cover may add organic material or nitrogen when managed correctly.

**Information gaps.** Exact flood depth, duration and recession date; whether a cover seed or inoculum is locally available; safe drainage; herbicide compatibility; labor, machinery, irrigation and market conditions; and which dry-season rice variety fits after the water recedes.

**Risks for local review.** Do not assume a cover will survive every flood or is locally adapted. Azolla needs managed standing water and can interfere with water movement if unmanaged. Do not plant dry-season rice unless the field is trafficable and irrigation is available. Review species choice, seed source and flood safety with a local technician.

## Common display rules

- Show every option as “could be considered; review with a local technician.”
- Label NASA POWER values as 2001–2025 climatology and zone-level data, not field measurements.
- Show missing field information as an information gap, never as zero.
- Label the El Niño adjustment as a scenario, not a forecast.
